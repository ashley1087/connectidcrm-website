What's a Core Message and Why Does Every Marketing Person Keep Telling Me I Need One?

By Ashley DePiano
August 20, 2026
7 min read

Someone asks what makes your HVAC company different from the one down the street. You have an answer ready. "We're reliable. Great customer service. We show up when we say we will." And so does every other company, on every other website, in every other market in the country. You know that when you're saying it. That's the problem.

The message isn't missing. It's just not doing anything.

What a core message actually is

One sentence. The one thing a customer should understand about your company after thirty seconds of exposure to your website, your truck wrap, your Google ad, whatever they see first. One sentence that makes it obvious who you're for and why you're the right call.

"Quality work at fair prices" doesn't count. That's the floor for being allowed to stay in business, not a reason to choose you.

A real core message is specific enough to describe a person. "We help homeowners in Westchester who've been burned by flaky contractors get HVAC work done on time, in writing, at a fixed price" tells you exactly who fits and exactly what they care about. Someone who isn't worried about flaky contractors and just wants the cheapest option might not connect with that. Good. That customer was going to be a problem anyway.

Why most home service businesses never wrote one

The early years of building a home service business are almost entirely word of mouth. A neighbor tells a neighbor. The experience does the talking. You never had to put it in writing because the referral network was doing it for you.

Then you start spending on marketing and suddenly you're trying to recreate that trust in thirty seconds with someone who's never heard of you. That requires a message. And most owners just skipped that step. Not on purpose. Nobody told them they needed it.

So the SEO company writes your meta descriptions based on keywords. The Google Ads person writes your headline copy based on what tests well in your industry. The social media manager picks a tone that feels on-brand to them. Everyone fills in the blank with something reasonable, and you end up with four different versions of your company scattered across your marketing, none of them quite right, none of them exactly wrong. Just not the same thing.

"Everyone fills in the blank with something reasonable. You end up with four different versions of your company and none of them are quite right."

What the absence of one actually costs you

Your ads say something about price. Your website says something about experience. Your GBP says something about your service area. A potential customer who sees all three comes away without a clear reason to call you over anyone else. They might still call. But they're also price-shopping, and they'll take the lower bid.

The other thing that happens is every new vendor you bring in has to invent your message from scratch. A new social media manager, a new agency, a new marketing coordinator — they don't have a brief, so they make one up. It's based on what they've seen work in your industry, which is the same stuff everyone in your industry is already saying. You end up sounding exactly like your competition because your competition's vendors made the same call yours did.

If this sounds like why your marketing spend hasn't been stacking, it usually is. Tactics can't compound toward a goal nobody defined.

How to actually write the sentence

The format that works: We help [who] who [specific worry] get [the real outcome] by [what makes you different].

Start with the "who," and go narrow. "Homeowners" isn't a who. Think about your three best jobs from the last year. What did those customers have in common? What were they worried about before they called? What problem did they have that your average competitor either couldn't or didn't bother to solve?

Then the outcome. Not the service — the thing underneath it. Homeowners don't want a new HVAC system. They want to not be sweating through July waiting three weeks for a tech who doesn't show up. The service is the vehicle. The outcome is what they actually care about.

The differentiator comes last, and it has to be true. If you're the only company in your market giving fixed-price quotes in writing, that's your differentiator. If you've worked the same fifteen zip codes for twenty years and know every building code inspector by name, say that. If your trucks and job sites are clean because you hired people who actually care about it, say it. Whatever your best customers mention when they recommend you is almost always your differentiator, sitting right there waiting.

What you do with it once you have it

You don't publish the sentence. That's not what it's for. It's a brief — an internal document that every person creating anything for your business reads before they start.

The Google Ads person reads it and knows what claim to lead with. The website person reads it and knows who the homepage hero should speak to. The social media manager reads it and knows what tone fits. Instead of four vendors inventing four versions of you, everyone's working from the same source of truth. The marketing starts to sound like it comes from one company with one reason to exist.

The other thing a core message does is make channel decisions easier. When someone pitches you on a new platform or a new tactic, the first question is whether that channel actually reaches the person you described in your message, and whether you can deliver it there. If yes, worth a conversation. If no, easy pass. That filter alone saves a lot of money.

If you haven't built your core message yet, there's a good chance you're also still figuring out whether you actually need a marketing strategy or just better tactics. The answer to both questions is the same thing: start with who you're for before you spend another dollar on how to reach them.
