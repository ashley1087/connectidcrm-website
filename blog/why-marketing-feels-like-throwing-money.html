Why Does My Marketing Feel Like I'm Just Throwing Money at Things?

By Ashley DePiano
August 11, 2026
7 min read

You signed the contract with the SEO agency on a Tuesday. By Thursday you were answering questions about keywords and target audiences you'd never really thought through. By month three you were getting reports full of numbers that didn't connect to anything you recognized as revenue. You kept paying because stopping felt like admitting it didn't work, and maybe it just needed more time.

Most marketing stories for home service businesses go exactly like that.

The money isn't the real problem. The problem is that you were buying tactics before you knew what your marketing was supposed to do, and nobody in the transaction had a reason to tell you.

You were buying tactics, not strategy, and nobody told you the difference

Every vendor you've hired came in and did their job. The SEO team chased rankings, the ads team chased clicks, and the social team chased engagement. Nobody was responsible for making it all point at the same thing, because there was no single thing defined. That's why it felt like throwing money. It kind of was.

Marketing works through compounding. An ad brings someone to your website, the website earns their trust, the follow-up sequence closes the job, and the happy customer leaves a review that brings in the next one. Every piece has to hand off to the next one cleanly, and that handoff only works when all the pieces are using the same language and aiming at the same person.

When you don't have a strategy, each piece gets optimized in isolation. The ads person targets a broad audience because narrow targeting is harder to defend in a monthly report. The SEO content covers questions your best customers never actually ask, because those questions have more search volume. The social posts go aspirational because that gets engagement, but your buyers are practical people with a specific problem to solve. None of that is a mistake exactly. It's what happens when capable people work without a shared picture of who you're for and what you need them to do.

Why all those vendor reports never connected to anything real

When you don't define success, vendors define it for you. SEO vendors track rankings and traffic. Ads vendors track impressions, clicks, and cost per lead. Social vendors track reach and engagement. These are all real numbers. They just might not connect to the one thing you actually care about, which is signed jobs.

That gap between "traffic went up 40 percent" and "revenue went up" is where most of your frustration lives. Filling it requires a strategy layer that nobody you hired was responsible for building. So you look at the reports, you can't find yourself in them, and you write another check because the alternative is admitting you don't know what you bought.

"When you don't define success, vendors define it for you. And they'll define it as whatever they can measure."

The agency didn't fail you. You didn't have what they needed.

The agencies you're frustrated with probably delivered what you hired them for. They got you traffic, or leads of some volume, or an SEO footprint that looks reasonable on paper. What they didn't do, and what you didn't hire them to do, was figure out who you actually are in the market. They didn't decide what makes a customer choose you over the company down the street. They didn't map what the real path looks like from someone first hearing your name to someone handing you a deposit.

Without that foundation, everyone fills in their own blanks. The SEO company writes your brand voice in your title tags. The ads team picks your differentiator based on what copy tests better in the creative rotation. The social manager invents a persona based on what they think your customers want to see. Everyone is guessing because nobody gave them something real to work from, and that includes you.

What's actually missing isn't a better vendor

Most owners ask which agency to try next. The more useful question is what would need to be true before any agency could actually help you.

You need a clear picture of who your best customer really is. Not "homeowners" or "residential clients" but a specific person with a specific problem at a specific moment who fits what you do well. You need a core message that explains why you in a way that isn't also true of every other company in your category. And you need a customer journey that maps how someone goes from not knowing you exist to calling you and then referring you to a neighbor.

Once those things exist, tactics start to work. The SEO content hits what your real buyers actually search. The ads reach the right person with something worth saying. The social posts connect to the sale instead of just generating likes. You can finally tell whether something is working because you know what working is supposed to look like. A lot of the frustration in "throwing money at marketing" comes from not having that picture. Without it, you can't steer and you can't tell when something's off.

The question that moves things from here
